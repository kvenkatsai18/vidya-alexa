# Cell Division: Biology Unit 4 Notes

## The cell cycle
Every cell that divides goes through a repeating sequence called the cell cycle. It has two main parts: interphase, when the cell grows and prepares, and the mitotic phase, when it actually divides. A typical cell spends about 90 percent of its life in interphase. Checkpoints between stages act like quality control, stopping the cycle if DNA is damaged or the cell isn't ready. When these checkpoints fail, cells can divide uncontrollably, which is how many cancers begin.

## Interphase and DNA replication
Interphase has three stages. In G1 the cell grows and does its normal job. In the S phase it copies all of its DNA, so each chromosome becomes two identical sister chromatids joined at a point called the centromere. In G2 the cell grows a little more and makes the proteins it needs for division. A common mistake is to think the cell is "resting" during interphase. It is actually very busy.

## Mitosis: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase
Mitosis divides the copied chromosomes equally between two new nuclei. Remember the order with PMAT. In prophase the chromosomes condense and become visible, the spindle fibres start to form, and the nuclear envelope breaks down. In metaphase the chromosomes line up single file along the middle of the cell, the metaphase plate. In anaphase the sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite ends of the cell. In telophase a new nuclear envelope forms around each set of chromosomes and they begin to unwind.

## Cytokinesis
Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm, and it usually overlaps with telophase. In animal cells the membrane pinches inward to form a cleavage furrow until the cell splits in two. Plant cells have a rigid cell wall, so instead they build a cell plate down the middle that becomes a new wall. The result is two daughter cells, each with a complete, identical set of chromosomes.

## Meiosis
Meiosis is a special division that makes gametes, the sperm and egg cells. It has two rounds of division, meiosis I and meiosis II, and produces four cells that each have half the usual number of chromosomes. These cells are called haploid. In prophase I, matching homologous chromosomes pair up and swap pieces of DNA in a process called crossing over. Meiosis I separates the homologous pairs, and meiosis II separates the sister chromatids. Because of crossing over and the random way pairs line up, every gamete is genetically unique.

## Mitosis versus meiosis
Mitosis is for growth and repair: one division, two daughter cells, each diploid and genetically identical to the parent. In humans that means 46 chromosomes in, 46 chromosomes out. Meiosis is for sexual reproduction: two divisions, four daughter cells, each haploid and genetically different. In humans that means 46 chromosomes in and 23 in each gamete. A quick memory trick: mitosis makes "my toes", ordinary body cells, while meiosis makes "me", the cells that could become a new person.
